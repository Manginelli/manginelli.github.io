About

Hi, I'm Daniel.

Game designer and lifelong player. I design games holistically, making mechanics, lore and feel work together to deliver what the game promises.

I've loved games since I was a kid, playing Street Fighter II on a Sega Genesis and kicking the air along with Ken. I never stopped. When I'm not working, I'm playing or watching video essays and reviews, trying to understand why a game works for some players and not for others, and whether it delivers the experience it promises.

The games that marked me most pulled me into their worlds in a way others couldn't: Metroid Prime, The Witcher 3, World of Warcraft. But I also love mechanical games: fighting games, roguelikes, the Dark Souls series.

I don't have a favorite genre. I have favorite games. And when I don't enjoy one, I assume I haven't understood it yet. The first time I played Dark Souls, it felt unfair and badly explained, because I was seeing it through the lens of other games instead of on its own terms. When I went back and listened to what it was trying to say, it became one of my favorite series.

There's no Daniel without games.

How I design

Every design decision answers to one question: what will the player experience?

It starts with an essence. Every game has a center that everything else takes its shape from: the experience at its heart. Fighting in Dark Souls and fighting in Devil May Cry are both combat, yet they feel nothing alike, because each grows from a different essence. To find it, I open with several ideas, dig into the strongest with references and possibilities, and narrow them down until one or a few are worth prototyping. How deep that funnel goes depends on the team, the time and the budget.

The core is built as one piece. Camera, controls and character exist to serve what the player does, so I design them together with the core action and start on game feel in the very first prototype. In Berserk Combat, the greatsword had to feel like a greatsword before the first demon showed up. I build these prototypes with Claude, which lets me test and iterate on ideas much faster.

Then I loop. Idea, prototype, play, read, adjust. Metrics tell me what happened; players tell me why. So I take care to understand who is giving each piece of feedback and where it comes from before acting on it. When the real problem is clear, the answer is usually obvious.

Every decision is a trade-off. No choice is better or worse on its own: it moves the game toward one experience or another, and the essence is what gives each choice its weight. Cohesion amplifies a good experience, and the worst sin is decisions that pull against each other, like a visual that promises something the gameplay never delivers. Interaction is the language of games, so what players see should be something they can play.

Done is a quality bar, not a feature count. A game with a constant quality bar is worth more than one packed with features, some polished and some raw. On Mars Incremental, I proposed cutting talent trees and buildings so we could ship at that bar.

Nobody owns the truth. I listen to each discipline, understand their why, and keep everyone on the same page through docs, reviews and playtests where the whole team plays the build.
